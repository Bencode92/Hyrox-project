## SECTION 1: PROGRESSIVE OVERLOAD IN FUNCTIONAL TRAINING

### 1.1 The Three Pillars of Functional Progressive Overload

Unlike barbell training, functional Hyrox exercises have three independent progression vectors that must be manipulated separately:
- **Load** (weight on sled, sandbag kg, kettlebell weight)
- **Volume** (distance, reps, sets, time under tension)
- **Specificity** (proximity to race conditions: fresh vs. fatigued, isolated vs. combined)

The key coaching principle: *build raw strength to handle the loads, then build muscular endurance to sustain them, then train strength in a fatigued state to replicate race demands.* These are three different adaptations requiring different programming.

---

### 1.2 Sandbag Exercise Progression

**Starting Point (Return from Injury / Beginner):**
- Begin at approximately 10% of bodyweight (e.g., 70 kg athlete = 7 kg sandbag)
- Master movement quality across full ROM before adding load
- Substitute: front rack walking lunges, Bulgarian split squats, tempo lunges (3-1-1)

**Weekly Progression Model:**
- Add 2–3 kg per week once technique holds under fatigue
- Do not progress weight until athlete can complete full race distance (100m) without form breakdown
- Target race weight 4–6 weeks before race day (race weights: Open Women 10 kg, Open Men 20 kg, Pro Women 20 kg, Pro Men 30 kg)

**The 4-Week Progressive Overload Cycle:**
- Week 1: 50% of race weight
- Week 2: 60% of race weight
- Week 3: 70% of race weight
- Week 4: 80% (or maximum manageable load)
- Retest and repeat cycle at elevated baseline

**Progression Variables in Order of Progression:**
1. Distance at sub-race weight (start 20m, build to 50m, then 100m)
2. Load increase (2–3 kg increments)
3. Fatigue state (perform after cardio, not fresh)
4. Race simulation (full 100m after 7 prior stations)

**Time Under Tension note:** Sandbag carries punish athletes with shoulder instability and spinal compression. Use 3-second eccentric on Bulgarian split squats as accessory work to develop positional strength.

---

### 1.3 Box Work Progression (Plyometric/Power)

Box work is not an official Hyrox station but is a critical training tool for developing power for sled work, burpees, and wall balls.

**Progression Ladder:**
1. **Step-ups** (no jump): Develop hip drive mechanics and unilateral strength
2. **Box step-up to knee drive**: Add hip flexor engagement and proprioception
3. **Low box jumps** (40–50 cm): Jump and step down; absorb with bent knees
4. **Standard box jumps** (50–60 cm): Jump and land softly; two-foot landing
5. **Depth drops** (step off, absorb): Train eccentric landing mechanics
6. **Depth jumps** (step off, immediately rebound): Reactive strength for burpee rebounds
7. **Weighted box step-ups**: Load with dumbbells or sandbag for carry-over to sled drive
8. **Lateral box jumps**: Power in multiple planes for sled turning

**Height Progression:** Increase box height only when athlete can land with full control (no knee valgus, soft landing, stable 2-second hold).

**Return from Injury:** Start with step-ups only. Progress to box jumps only when single-leg hop test shows symmetric power output. Reactive plyometrics (depth jumps) come last.

---

### 1.4 Bodyweight Exercise Progression (Burpee Broad Jumps)

The burpee broad jump is the only Hyrox station that is pure bodyweight, but it demands progressive training like any loaded movement.

**Progression Ladder:**
1. **Slow burpees** (no jump): Master hip hinge, plank position, step-up technique
2. **Standard burpees** (jump up): Full movement at low intensity
3. **Burpee + step-up technique** (efficiency focus): Step one foot at a time instead of both (lower HR spike, preferred by elite athletes)
4. **Burpee + broad jump** (submaximal distance): Short jumps focusing on arm swing
5. **Burpee broad jumps** at race pace for 10–20m
6. **Burpee broad jumps** for 40m, then 60m, then 80m unbroken
7. **Compromised BBJ** (after 1 km run or prior station): Race simulation
8. **Full 80m under fatigue**: Complete simulation

**Volume Progression:**
- Week 1–2: 3×10 reps, full rest between
- Week 3–4: 3×15m continuous
- Week 5–6: 3×30m continuous
- Week 7–8: 2×60m with form focus
- Race prep: 1×80m timed, after prior station effort

**Efficiency key (elite coaching cue):** "Resist the urge to use maximal power on the jump—it burns lactate fast and gives minimal extra distance." Arms generate distance without leg cost; use them aggressively.

### 1.5 Carry Progressions (Farmers Carry)

**The Progression Hierarchy (three variables to manipulate):**

| Priority | Variable | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | Distance at light load | Establish technique, grip, posture |
| 2nd | Weight increase | Build raw carrying strength |
| 3rd | Race weight for full distance | Specificity |
| 4th | Overload training (heavier than race weight) | Relative strength reserve |

**Specific Model:**
- Start: 15% of bodyweight per hand (70 kg athlete = ~10 kg per hand)
- Add 2–3 kg per hand per week
- Smart programming mixes:
  - Heavy carries: 5×40m at race weight or 4–8 kg heavier, short distance (30–50m)
  - Endurance carries: 3×30m at race weight with deliberate cadence
  - Race carries: 1×200m at race weight under fatigue

**Grip as limiting factor:** If grip fails before legs or lungs, develop grip separately: dead hangs (3×30–45s), towel/fat-grip holds (3×20s), Romanian deadlifts (4×10), suitcase carries for unilateral stability.

**Race weights:** Open Women 2×16 kg, Open Men 2×24 kg, Pro Women 2×24 kg, Pro Men 2×32 kg.

### 1.6 Sled Work Progressions

**Rich Ryan's Three-Pillar Sled Model (elite-level framework):**
1. **Strength component:** Heavy loads, short distances (≤20m sets); builds raw force production
2. **Speed component:** Lighter loads at maximum velocity; builds neuromuscular power transfer
3. **Lactate tolerance component:** Moderate load, continuous effort; builds capacity to handle metabolic byproducts

**Undulated (non-linear) progression is preferred over linear:** Rotate between heavy/short, moderate/fast, lighter/long sessions within the same week rather than adding weight each week.

**Frequency:** Two dedicated sled sessions weekly maximum. More risks calf/Achilles fatigue and Achilles tendinopathy.

**Session Structure:**
- Session 1 (Strength): 4×20m heavy pushes; rest 2–3 min; accessory work (split squats, leg press)
- Session 2 (Varied): 6×10m sprints light load + 3×50m at race weight; rest 90s between

**Progressive Training Volumes (6-week build):**
- Weeks 1–2: 50–70% race weight, ≤150m total sled volume per session
- Weeks 3–4: 70–85% race weight, up to 200m total
- Weeks 5–6: 100% race weight, occasional overload at 110–120%

**Important for return from injury:** Sled work is unique in rehabilitation—it has no eccentric loading component, making it safe for earlier return than most lower-body exercises. Sled work can be programmed over varying distances with a variety of loads and can begin during recovery phases under pain-free conditions.

**Taper:** Reduce sled frequency 3 weeks pre-competition. No sled work in the final 5 days before race.

### 1.7 Specific Progression Models Used by Hyrox Coaches

**Model A – Rich Ryan (RMR Training) – Threshold-First Model:**
All progression measured by time accumulated in Zone 4 (anaerobic threshold), not by weight or reps alone. Sessions start at 7–7.5/10 RPE and progress to 8–8.5/10. Peak: 100 minutes/week in Zone 4 split across two sessions. Station-specific work placed inside threshold efforts, not treated separately.

**Model B – Jake Dearden (FSF) – Race-Simulation Model:**
Three 15-minute AMRAP blocks per week, each starting with a 1000m run, followed by race-specific station exercises. Progressive overload applied via increasing rounds completed per time block (not by adding weight). Six sessions/week total: one rest day.

**Model C – Pedro Tirado (Paradox) – Hybrid Bodybuilding Model:**
6 days/week: 3 bodybuilding (Push/Pull/Legs, 6–10 rep range with progressive overload), 2 Hyrox-specific conditioning (high-intensity circuits), 1 full-body hybrid. Progressive overload applied via RPE scaling. 12-week arc with weeks 9–10 as peak intensity.

**Model D – Rich Ryan / TrainRox – Block Periodization:**
3-cycle model (strength → muscular endurance → aerobic/Hyrox-specific), with 4-week mesocycles and a deload every 4th week. Station work integrated into conditioning phase, not strength phase.

## SECTION 2: RECOVERY & ADAPTATION FOR HYROX

### 2.1 Recovery Between Functional Training Sessions

**General Rule:** Leave 24–48 hours between training the same movement pattern or muscle group.

**Practical Application by Session Type:**
| Session Type | Minimum Recovery Before Same Pattern |
|---|---|
| Heavy sled (RPE 8–9) | 48–72 hours |
| Race simulation / full Hyrox workout | 72–96 hours |
| Station-specific technique work (RPE 6–7) | 24 hours |
| Zone 2 running + light carries | 16–24 hours |
| Compromised running blocks | 48 hours |

**The CNS drain problem:** High-intensity functional work (RPE 8+) drains the central nervous system more than pure cardio. DOMS (delayed onset muscle soreness) resolves in 2–3 days, but CNS fatigue can linger 5–7 days after maximal sessions. Athletes often feel physically fine but see declining performance—this is the CNS.

---

### 2.2 Managing Fatigue from High-Intensity Functional Work

**The Unique Fatigue Challenge of Hyrox:**
Athletes must manage both metabolic fatigue (sustained aerobic demand) and neuromuscular fatigue (functional stations). These interact differently than in pure strength or pure endurance sports:

- After sled push: local leg fatigue + lactate spike, but HR may recover quickly
- After wall balls: cardiorespiratory demand dominates, less local fatigue
- After farmers carry: grip fatigue precedes cardiovascular fatigue
- After burpee broad jumps: systemic fatigue + shoulder fatigue + lactate

**The Gray Zone Problem:** Most Hyrox athletes spend too much time at moderate intensity (Zone 3), creating fatigue without driving adaptation. Polarized training (80% Zone 2, 20% Zone 4–5) produces faster improvements.

**Practical fatigue management rule:** If RPE for a familiar session rises by more than 1.5 points without increased load, fatigue has accumulated. Adjust session down by 20% or swap for Zone 2 work.

---

### 2.3 Heart Rate Recovery as an Indicator

**HRV (Heart Rate Variability):**
- HRV provides daily readiness data. A drop of 10%+ from rolling 7-day average = reduce training intensity by 20–30%
- Two consecutive low HRV days = mandatory light session or rest
- Two+ warning signs (fatigue lingering + motivation dips + HRV drops) = trigger a Restoration Week

**During-Race HR Management:**
- Target race effort at Zone 4 (80–90% max HR) throughout
- Stations will spike HR above 90%—this is expected and unavoidable
- Running segments serve as HR recovery: goal is to bring HR back to Zone 3–4 threshold within 60–90 seconds of starting run
- Start race controlled for 10–12 minutes—hold back deliberately
- Never rely solely on HR during functional stations: when grip fails during farmers carry, HR may still show Zone 3 but the athlete is at local RPE 9

**Heart Rate Zones (practical reference):**

| Zone | % Max HR | Description | Training Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zone 1 | 50–60% | Active recovery | Cool-downs, deload days |
| Zone 2 | 60–70% | Conversational pace | Base building, 80% of volume |
| Zone 3 | 70–80% | "Gray zone"—avoid overuse | Tempo work, aerobic intervals |
| Zone 4 | 80–90% | Hard but repeatable threshold | Race pace, 20% of training |
| Zone 5 | 90–100% | Maximum, unsustainable | Short intervals, speed work |

---

### 2.4 When to Push vs. When to Back Off

**Push if:**
- Weekly fatigue score remains manageable (using sets × reps formula with RPE)
- Sleep quality is 7+ hours with low disturbance
- HRV within 10% of rolling 7-day average
- Performance has been improving or holding stable
- This is an accumulation week in the training block

**Back off if:**
- RPE rises without additional load
- DOMS lasting more than 3 days
- HRV drops 10%+ from rolling average
- Sleep quality declining
- Training block calls for deload (every 4th week)
- Multiple consecutive sessions above RPE 8.5

**Autoregulation swap model:** On low-readiness days, do not skip. Instead, downgrade: swap heavy sled for technique-only sled (60% load), swap race simulation for station practice at 60–70% effort, swap interval run for Zone 2.

---

### 2.5 Specific Recovery Protocols Between Hyrox Station Training

**Within a training session (between station blocks):**
- After heavy sled sets: 2–3 minutes full rest before next station
- Between compromised running blocks: 90 seconds active recovery (walk)
- After max-effort simulation: 5+ minutes before repeating same circuit

**Post-Session Recovery Protocol (72-Hour Framework):**
- Hours 0–24: Passive recovery—electrolytes, 20–40g protein, gentle stretching
- Hours 24–48: Active recovery—walking, swimming, Zone 1 cycling, foam rolling
- Hours 48–72: Light movement and nutrition recharge

**Deload week (every 4th week):** Reduce total volume by 40–50%. Maintain some intensity (2–3 brief Zone 4 efforts) but eliminate high-volume functional work. This is when long-term gains are consolidated, not lost.

---

### 2.6 Nutrition Timing Around Functional Sessions

**Pre-Session (functional training):**
- 1–2 hours before (light session): Small meal, carbs + protein (e.g., banana + Greek yogurt)
- 2–3 hours before (race simulation): Full meal—complex carbs + lean protein + low fat/fiber

**Carbohydrate needs by training volume:**
- 3–5 hrs/week training: 3–5 g/kg bodyweight/day
- 6–12 hrs/week: 5–8 g/kg/day
- 12+ hrs/week: 8–12 g/kg/day

**Protein targets:** 1.6–2.4 g per kg of lean body mass/day. Post-workout: 20–40g high-quality protein within 30–60 minutes.

**Post-Session window:** Refuel within 30–60 minutes. Formula: bodyweight (kg) × 1.5 = grams of carbohydrate needed in first post-workout meal. Follow with full meal within 2 hours.

**Hydration:** 3+ liters daily with electrolytes during training. Dehydrated muscles are significantly more prone to cramping and tearing.

---

### 2.7 Sleep and Functional Performance

Athletes sleeping 10 hours outperform those sleeping 6 hours across all performance metrics. Even sub-optimal sleep of 6 hours shows sharp performance decline within 2 weeks. Sleep is the foundation of all recovery: without it, no other protocol compensates.

**Sleep targets for Hyrox athletes:**
- Minimum: 8 hours per night
- Optimal: 8.5–9 hours, especially in high-volume training weeks
- Post-race simulation or race day: prioritize 9–10 hours

Elite athletes tracked on HRV platforms show optimal HRV scores consistently require 8.5+ hour sleep windows.

---

### 2.8 Signs of Overtraining Specific to Hyrox Athletes

**Early warning (take action):**
- RPE rising on familiar workouts without increased load
- Heavy legs persisting beyond 48 hours post-session
- Declining motivation or training dread
- Sleep quality deteriorating despite tiredness
- Small joint niggles (especially knee, Achilles, shoulder) not resolving

**Overtraining syndrome (require intervention):**
- Performance times declining across multiple metrics simultaneously
- Persistent fatigue not responding to 2–3 days rest
- Increased susceptibility to illness (immune suppression)
- Emotional irritability or apathy
- HRV consistently below 7-day average without recovery
- DOMS lasting 3+ days after normal-intensity sessions

**Root cause of Hyrox overtraining:** The most common pattern is excessive time in Zone 3 (moderate intensity) combined with heavy station work. Athletes underestimate the accumulated cost of running + functional exercises versus pure lifting or pure running. The hybrid demand is cumulative and not intuitive.

### 3.5 Tapering Functional Work Before Race

**Two-Week Taper (recommended for most athletes):**

**Week 1 of Taper:**
- **Volume:** 60–70% of normal week
- **Running:** Shorter (4–6 km) with 30–60 second race-pace strides
- **Stations:** Drastically reduced reps/rounds (e.g., 3 rounds of 5 BBJ vs. normal 5 rounds of 10)
- **Strength:** Reduce weight 20–30%, fewer sets, form focus only

**Week 2 of Taper (race week):**
- **Mon–Tue:** 40–50% of normal volume; short 15–20 second race-pace bursts; bodyweight station technique only
- **Wed–Thu:** 3–4 km easy running with 2–3 brief intense bursts; optional very light mobility
- **Fri:** 15–20 min easy walk or gentle stretching only
- **Sat (race eve):** No training; 5–10 minute walk only

**What to keep:**
- Brief race-pace rehearsals (neuromuscular sharpness)
- Familiar movement patterns at very low volume
- Mobility and hydration focus

**What to cut completely:**
- High-volume sets or rounds
- New exercises or recovery tools
- Heavy lifting
- Any session causing significant DOMS risk

**Critical mistake to avoid:** Do not introduce any new exercises, equipment, massage styles, or cold plunge routines. The week before race, the work is done—the goal is removing accumulated fatigue, not adding fitness.

## SECTION 4: RPE & AUTOREGULATION IN FUNCTIONAL TRAINING

### 4.1 How to Use RPE for Sandbag/Sled/Box Work

**RPE in Hyrox functional training is fundamentally different from barbell RPE** because:
- Local muscular fatigue often peaks before cardiovascular fatigue (e.g., grip fails before heart rate spikes)
- The "fatigue" signal can come from an entirely different body system than the one being trained
- Cumulative session fatigue changes RPE dramatically across a workout that starts fresh

**Practical RPE Guide for Hyrox Movements:**

| RPE | Description | Application |
|---|---|---|
| 5–6 | Easy, full conversation possible | Technique practice, recovery sessions |
| 7 | Challenging but sustainable; can speak short sentences | Volume accumulation, endurance sets |
| 7.5 | Starting to tax the system; preferred training intensity | Base training sessions |
| 8 | Hard; very difficult to speak; could do 2–3 more reps | Build phase sessions |
| 8.5 | Near-limit; 1–2 reps/sets left in reserve | Peak phase sessions |
| 9–10 | Race effort; maximum sustainable, then redline | Race simulation only |

**RPE targets from Rich Ryan's protocol:**
- Base training: begin sessions at 7–7.5/10, allow natural rise to 8–8.5/10 by end
- Never regularly train at 9–10/10—accumulate volume at 8–8.5 to drive adaptation without destroying recovery

**Station-Specific RPE Calibration:**
- Sled push training sets: RPE 7–8 with load allowing 50m in 60–90 seconds
- Sandbag lunges: RPE 7 for technique sets, 8–8.5 for race-simulation sets
- Farmers carry: RPE 7 at race weight for full 200m; RPE 8+ for overload training
- Wall balls: RPE 7 for rhythm sets, 8.5 for race-pace sets (final 20–25 reps will feel like 9+)

---

### 4.2 Autoregulation Models for Circuit Training

**The Fatiguability Score Model (quantified autoregulation):**

Formula: Sets × (Reps^0.65) × (RPE/10) × Exercise Fatigue Coefficient

**Exercise Fatigue Coefficient Reference:**
- Sled push/pull: HIGH (lower body + systemic)
- Sandbag lunges: HIGH (spinal compression + unilateral fatigue)
- Burpee broad jumps: HIGH (total body + cardiovascular)
- Farmers carry: MODERATE (grip-limited, core-heavy)
- Wall balls: MODERATE-HIGH (lower body + cardiorespiratory)
- SkiErg: MODERATE (upper body + cardiorespiratory)
- Rowing: MODERATE (full body but seated)

**Weekly Fatigue Score Ranges:**
- 0–200: Under-trained or deload week
- 200–260: Optimal for intermediate athletes
- 260–280: Accumulation phase (sustainable 1–2 weeks with recovery)
- 280+: Risk of overreaching—trigger rest or swap sessions

**Practical autoregulation for circuits:**
- On low-readiness days: reduce RPE target by 1 point and/or reduce sets by 1
- Do not skip entirely—movement quality maintenance matters
- Swap high-fatigability exercises (sled + lunges same day) for moderate alternatives (SkiErg + carries)

### 4.3 When RPE is Misleading in Functional Work

**The Core Problem: Cardio Fatigue vs. Muscular Fatigue**

In Hyrox training, these two types of fatigue diverge dramatically:

1. **Cardiovascular RPE** — how taxed your aerobic system is (breathing, heart rate, blood lactate)
2. **Muscular/local RPE** — how fatigued a specific muscle or joint is

**Critical examples of RPE mismatch:**
- **Farmers carry:** Grip fails at muscular RPE 9 while cardiovascular RPE is still 6. The athlete can breathe perfectly but cannot maintain grip. Solution: program grip accessory work separately; do not confuse grip failure with full fatigue.
- **Sled push:** Calves and Achilles reach local RPE 8 after repeated sessions, even when cardiovascular system is fresh and overall RPE feels like 6. This is the mechanism behind Achilles injury in over-trained sled athletes.
- **SkiErg:** Athletes with poor lat/pulling strength will reach local muscular RPE 9 in arms while heart rate barely touches Zone 3.
- **Burpee Broad Jumps:** Cardiovascular RPE spikes to 9 rapidly while legs are not actually taxed—creating the illusion of maximal effort when legs have more capacity.

**From the 2025 research study:**
- Functional station RPE averaged 18/20 (Borg scale)
- Running segment RPE averaged 16/20
- Wall balls (final station) produced highest lactate AND highest RPE of the entire race
- This means: athletes who rely on RPE as the only fatigue indicator during stations will systemically underestimate the accumulated fatigue going into the final station

**Coaching implication:** Teach athletes to distinguish "local failure" from "global fatigue." When a body part fails, that is a local event. When the whole system is shutting down (shortness of breath, tunnel vision, lack of coordination), that is global fatigue. Training should address whichever is the limiting factor, not just train around it.

### 4.4 Distinguishing "Good Tired" from "Bad Tired"

**Good Tired (productive fatigue):**
- Arrives gradually during a planned high-load session
- Feels earned and proportional to the effort invested
- DOMS resolves within 24–48 hours
- Performance returns to baseline or above within 48–72 hours
- Athlete is mentally motivated for the next session
- HRV drops briefly but returns to normal within 1–2 days

**Bad Tired (counterproductive fatigue / early overtraining):**
- Feels disproportionate to the training load
- DOMS lasts 3+ days
- Performance does not recover to baseline between sessions
- Motivation to train is absent or requires significant effort
- HRV consistently below 7-day average
- New joint niggles appear (especially knees, Achilles, lower back)
- Sleep quality deteriorates

**The Autoregulation Decision Tree for Coaches:**
```
Is the athlete tired?
├── YES: Is motivation to train still present?
│   ├── YES: Check if performance is holding → TRAIN at 80% intensity
│   └── NO: Check HRV and sleep quality
│       ├── Both normal → Emotional dip, mild deload (Zone 2 only)
│       └── HRV dropped OR sleep poor → MANDATORY rest or active recovery only
└── NO: Is this consistent across 7+ days?
    └── YES: Risk of undertraining; increase stimulus
```

## SECTION 5: INJURY PREVENTION & RETURN TO TRAINING

### 5.1 The Five Most Common Hyrox Injuries

**1. Knee Pain (Patellofemoral/Patellar Tendinopathy)**
- **Mechanism:** Running under fatigue + repeated wall ball squats + deep lunges = cumulative patellar stress
- **Prevention:** Hip/glute strengthening (single-leg RDL, hip thrusts), ankle mobility work, gait coaching
- **Return Protocol:** Movement pattern assessment → corrective exercises → loaded strength work (no impact) → running reintroduction

**2. Low Back Strains**
- **Mechanism:** Sled push with rounded lumbar spine, heavy carries with faulty form under fatigue, sandbag lunges with forward lean
- **Prevention:** Dynamic core bracing (anti-rotation work, Pallof press, planks with reach), glute/hamstring strength, hip hinge mastery
- **Return Protocol:** Manual therapy → targeted core + hip strength → sled at 50% load before full return

**3. Achilles Tendon Irritation / Tendinopathy**
- **Mechanism:** Rapid volume increases in running + sled work + burpee rebounds. Sled push specifically overloads the Achilles via isometric loading through the calf.
- **Prevention:** Progressive plyometric loading, eccentric heel drops (3×15 daily), ankle mobility work
- **Return Protocol:** Eccentric loading programs → guided return-to-run → sled reintroduction last

**4. Shoulder Pain (Rotator Cuff / Overuse)**
- **Mechanism:** Wall balls with limited overhead mobility, rowing with forward head posture, farmers carry with weak scapular stabilizers
- **Prevention:** Scapular stabilizer work (band pull-aparts, face pulls), rotator cuff strengthening, thoracic mobility
- **Return Protocol:** Joint mobilization → manual therapy → strength-based rehab (not return to wall balls until pain-free overhead movement achieved)

**5. Plantar Fasciitis / Foot Pain**
- **Mechanism:** Running on hard surfaces under fatigue, tight calves, weak foot stabilizers
- **Prevention:** Foot arch strengthening, calf flexibility, proper footwear selection (cushioned running shoes for runs, cross-training shoes for stations)
- **Return Protocol:** Soft tissue release → foot stability drills → gradual running volume reintroduction

### 5.2 Safe Return to Sled Work, Box Jumps, Burpees After Injury

**The Four-Phase Return Model (evidence-based):**

**Phase 1 – Tissue Protection (immediate post-injury):**
- Minimal loading; maintain movement quality
- Primary goals: pain reduction, inflammation control, prevent muscle inhibition
- Alternative training: SkiErg + rowing (upper body bias) for lower limb injuries; running + lower stations for shoulder injuries
- Loading: 0–40% of pre-injury levels

**Phase 2 – Early Loading (~50–60% of pre-injury level):**
- Systematic 5–10% weekly increases
- Strength target: reach 60% of uninjured side before progression
- Sled: begin sled work (no eccentric = low injury risk) at 50% load
- Box work: step-ups only (no jumps)
- Burpees: slow movement practice (no jump components)
- Do not exceed 15% total load increase per week—above 15% creates significantly elevated re-injury risk

**Phase 3 – Progressive Strengthening (70–80% baseline):**
- Loading approaches 70–80% of pre-injury capacity
- Box jumps can be introduced: low box, land and step down (no reactive jump)
- Burpees: add jump-up component, still no broad jump
- Sled: approach race weight at reduced distance

**Phase 4 – Return to Performance (80%+ of previous capability):**
- Sport-specific movement patterns at full load
- Reactive plyometrics (depth drops → depth jumps)
- Full burpee broad jumps for distance
- Full sled at race weight for race distance
- Full station simulation under fatigue

**Sled-specific return note:** Sled work is uniquely suited for rehabilitation because it has no eccentric loading. Eccentric loading is the primary driver of muscle damage. Sled work can begin earlier in rehab than most lower-body exercises and can progress through load variation without the eccentric-load concern.

**Key principle:** "The goal of injury management is not to pause training; it is to modify it." Maintain cardiovascular fitness throughout via low-impact alternatives (cycling, SkiErg, rowing, swimming)—the aerobic adaptations transfer significantly.

---

### 5.3 Mobility and Prehab Protocols Specific to Hyrox

**The Four Key Mobility Areas for Hyrox:**

**1. Hip Mobility (Critical for ALL stations)**
- Restricted hip flexion, extension, and external rotation limits stride length, squat depth, and sled power output
- Daily exercises: 90/90 hip stretch, dynamic lunge variations, band-resisted hip openers, deep squat holds

**2. Ankle Dorsiflexion (Sled Push + Running)**
- Limited ankle flexion reduces sled push power and alters running biomechanics under fatigue
- Exercises: Wall ankle test (daily), ankle hops, eccentric calf work, ankle circles
- Test: Knee-to-wall test—if knee cannot touch wall with heel flat at 10 cm distance, dorsiflexion is a limiter

**3. Thoracic Spine Mobility (Rowing + SkiErg + Wall Balls)**
- Stiff thoracic spine forces lumbar compensation → low back injury risk
- Exercises: Foam roller thoracic extensions, cat-cow, thoracic rotation stretches
- Test: Seated thoracic rotation—can you reach 40° rotation each side?

**4. Shoulder Mobility (Wall Balls + Farmers Carry + Rowing)**
- Limited internal rotation and overhead range creates rotator cuff overload
- Exercises: Sleeper stretch (internal rotation), PVC pass-throughs, wall slides (scapular control)
- Test: Can you achieve full overhead position (ear between arms) without lower back arching?

**Daily Prehab Routine (10 minutes, pre-session):**
1. 90/90 hip stretch — 60 seconds each side
2. Wall ankle mobilization — 15 reps each
3. Thoracic foam roller — 10 extensions
4. Band pull-aparts — 2×20
5. Deep squat hold — 60 seconds
6. Dynamic lunges with thoracic rotation — 10 each side
7. Arm circles + shoulder circles — 10 each direction

---

### 5.4 Red Flags During Functional Training

**Stop immediately and seek assessment if:**
- Sharp, stabbing pain (not burning/fatigue) at any joint during movement
- Sudden "pop" or "snap" sensation in any joint or tendon
- Acute weakness in a limb during a station (sudden inability to control weight)
- Dizziness or near-blackout during or after station work
- Numbness or tingling radiating down an arm or leg
- Pain that worsens during activity rather than warming up and settling

**Address within 48 hours if:**
- Joint pain (not muscle soreness) persisting beyond 24 hours
- Swelling around any joint post-session
- Persistent tightness that does not resolve with warm-up
- Performance asymmetry (one side failing before the other in a bilateral exercise)

**Address at next coaching session if:**
- DOMS lasting 3+ days
- Grip failing earlier than expected on carries
- Form breakdown occurring at weights previously manageable
- Footstrike pattern changing during fatigued running

## SECTION 6: SPECIFIC COACHING CUES FOR HYROX STATIONS

### 6.1 Sled Push — 50m (Open Men: 152 kg / Open Women: 102 kg)

**Optimal Body Position:**
- 45-degree forward lean: drives horizontal force through the sled (not vertical pressure down)
- Hands grip poles just below shoulder height
- Elbows soft and bent (not locked): tension locks decrease force transfer efficiency
- Forearms flush against poles for maximum contact and control
- Core braced in a plank position: connects upper and lower body force chain
- Never hyperextend through the lumbar spine

**Step Length and Foot Mechanics:**
- Start with staggered stance to build initial momentum
- Transition to short, fast, choppy steps once moving
- Keep feet low to the ground—maintain constant traction
- Do NOT overstride: overstriding breaks contact and loses traction
- Test both short/quick steps and longer strides in training—individual biomechanics vary

**The Three-Phase Drive Rhythm:**
1. Acceleration phase (first 5–10m): Build momentum gradually from static
2. Steady drive (mid-range): Maintain constant pressure with rhythmic steps
3. Regrip and reset (brief pauses if needed): Controlled breath, rebrace core, drive again

**Breathing Cue:** Brace and hold for 3–5 steps, then exhale sharply during a brief reset. Do not hold breath for the entire 50m.

**Pacing Targets:**
- Open Division: 50m in 1:45–2:00
- Pro Division: 50m in 2:00–2:30 (heavier loads)

**Training Frequency:** Twice weekly maximum. More creates Achilles/calf overuse. Integrate sled push as the first functional station in compromised running sessions (not after other lower-body work).

**Key coaching cue:** "Lean into the earth, not into the sled. Drive the ground away with your legs—the sled follows."

---

### 6.2 Sled Pull — 50m (Open Men: 102 kg / Open Women: 72 kg)

**Two Primary Techniques:**

**Option A – Anchor and Reel:**
- Stand upright, weight anchored back into hips (deadlift posture)
- Arms bent at ~90 degrees; pull rope hand-over-hand
- Hip slightly higher or lower depending on mobility
- Use body weight as anchor; arm action pulls rope while legs hold position

**Option B – Backward Walk:**
- Get low; pull rope toward you while walking backward
- Core tight; drive through legs (not just arms)
- Keep flat back throughout

**Hybrid Technique (recommended):** Begin with hip-drive anchor pull, then transition to backward walk as rope runs out and sled approaches.

**Key Coaching Cues:**
- "Sit back into your hips like a deadlift, prouder chest"
- "Drive through your legs—not just your arms"
- "Keep your core tight; don't let your back round"
- When using rope hand-over-hand: "Maintain tension at all times—slack is wasted energy"

**Pacing Strategy:** Resist the urge to sprint at the start. Begin with steady, strong pulls to maintain rhythm. Short planned rest breaks after every 1–2 sled lengths (if needed) allow recovery without losing momentum.

**Training without a sled:** Substitute with bent-over rows, seated cable rows, RDLs, farmer hinges. For grip-specific carryover: rope pulls, towel pull-ups, dead hangs.

---

### 6.3 Wall Balls — 100 reps (Men 9 kg @ 3m target / Women 6 kg @ 3m target)

**Setup and Stance:**
- Stand arm's length from wall
- Feet shoulder-width apart, toes slightly out
- Hold ball at chest height with elbows under wrists
- Heels grounded throughout

**Squat Mechanics:**
- Descend until hip crease passes below knees (this is the judged standard; 15 or 30-second penalties for shallow reps)
- Maintain braced core and neutral spine
- Drive powerfully through glutes upward

**Power Source (Critical coaching cue):** "Use your legs and hips, not your arms, to generate the throw height." Leg drive precedes arm extension. The ball should reach the target via hip extension + leg drive; arms only guide the final trajectory.

**Throw Timing:**
- Catch ball as you descend into the squat (use the momentum of descent)
- "Catch in a low position—let the ball's weight help initiate your next squat"
- Arm extension synchronized with upward leg drive

**Breathing Rhythm:**
- Inhale during descent
- Exhale as you drive and throw
- Recovery breaths: if HR spikes, pause for two deep breaths every 10–15 reps before form deteriorates

**Rep Strategy by Ability:**

| Level | Set Structure | Rest |
|---|---|---|
| Beginner | 10×10 reps | 10 seconds |
| Intermediate | 5×20 reps | 15–20 seconds |
| Advanced | 3×33 + 1 final rep | 5–10 seconds |
| Elite | Unbroken 100 | No planned rest |

**Key coaching cues:**
- "Think rhythm, not reps—every throw should feel like a metronome"
- "Strong legs, steady breath, one rep at a time"
- "Catch softly with hands—don't fight the ball"
- "Start conservative for the first 50; the last 30 are mental"

**2025/26 rule update:** 15–30 second penalties for short squats or missed targets. Judges watch every rep. In training, use a depth box or marker to ensure consistent depth.

---

### 6.4 Burpee Broad Jumps — 80m

**Step-Up vs. Jump-Up:**
- **Elite athletes predominantly use the step-up technique** (step one foot forward, then the other—not jump with both feet simultaneously)
- Jump-up: higher HR spike, more energy cost, faster but unsustainable
- Step-up: lower HR, more efficient, better for maintaining rhythm across 80m

**Body Position and Mechanics:**
- Chest-to-floor burpee position (mandatory for judging)
- Stand fully before the jump (hips and knees extended)
- **Arms:** Swing arms aggressively forward and upward during the jump—"arms add serious distance without extra leg cost"
- **Jump:** Submaximal effort (70–80% power)—"resist the urge to use maximal power; it burns lactate fast and adds minimal distance"
- **Landing:** Absorb with bent knees; soft landing position prevents knee stress and reduces ground contact time

**Pacing Strategy:**
- Do NOT sprint for distance on each individual jump
- Establish a sustainable rhythm that you can hold for all 80m
- The station is only 80m; it takes most athletes 45–60 reps (variable stride length)
- Start controlled for first 20–30m; settle into rhythm; maintain to end

**Data insight:** Near-zero correlation between BBJ speed and subsequent running performance—athletes who attack BBJ aggressively do not run slower on the following run segment. This suggests athletes are leaving time on the table by being overly conservative.

**Training Progressions:**
- Phase 1: Burpees only (technique)
- Phase 2: Burpees + jump (no distance target)
- Phase 3: 10–20m sets focusing on arm swing
- Phase 4: Full 40m, then 60m, then 80m
- Phase 5: Compromised BBJ (after 1 km run or prior stations)

---

### 6.5 Farmers Carry — 200m

**Setup:**
- Pick up weights using a hip hinge (not a squat)
- Stand tall: shoulders over hips, neutral spine, chest open
- Arms hang naturally at sides; bells close to body
- Head neutral, eyes forward

**Walking Mechanics:**
- Short, fast steps using heel-to-toe pattern
- Avoid side sway or leaning to either side
- Minimize weight swinging during movement
- Maintain even bilateral pressure

**Grip Strategy:**
- Grip firmly in the center of each handle
- Relax fingers slightly between steps to prevent forearm pump
- Maintain even grip pressure both sides
- Under grip fatigue: shake out one hand at a time (not both)—maintains momentum

**Breathing:**
- Inhale through nose; exhale through pursed lips in short rhythmic patterns
- Do not breath-hold—tension without airflow causes early fatigue
- Controlled rhythmic breathing maintains intra-abdominal pressure effectively

**Pacing Targets:**
- Open Division: 200m in 1:20–1:45
- Pro Division: 200m in 1:10–1:30

**Training Overload Strategy:**
- Heavy carries: 5×40m at 4–8 kg above race weight
- Endurance carries: 3×200m at race weight
- Grip development: dead hangs, suitcase carries, Romanian deadlifts

---

### 6.6 Sandbag Lunges — 100m

**Carry Position:**
- Sandbag across the shoulders (mandatory per Hyrox rules)
- Both feet behind the start line before first step
- Upper back engaged to support the load; do not collapse forward

**Lunge Mechanics (Judged Standards):**
- Back knee must touch the ground every rep
- Both knees and hips fully extended at the top
- Front foot stable and planted
- Do not drop the sandbag—dropping wastes time and energy on pickup

**Step Strategy:**
- **Optimal stride length: 0.9–1.1 meters** (data-driven)—reduces total reps to 100–110 and shifts demand toward glutes/hamstrings (preserving quads for wall balls)
- Short steps shift demand to quads (which are already fatigued and needed for wall balls)
- Stay tall through torso; do not lean forward excessively
- Short stride with knee kissing the floor = consistent depth, less balance energy wasted

**Pacing Strategy (counterintuitive):**
- Data from 134 athletes shows near-zero correlation between sandbag lunge speed and Run 8 performance
- Sandbag lunges DO correlate strongly with wall ball performance (r=0.738)—technique on lunges directly impacts the next station
- **Coaching recommendation:** Attack the lunges; do not conserve. Continuous movement is faster—every stop means re-accelerating the sandbag
- Start slightly conservative for first 20–30m, then settle into sustainable unbroken rhythm
- Athletes can gain 60–90 seconds through biomechanical optimization (stride length, posture) rather than intensity modulation

**Training Volume:**
- 2–3 sessions per week, mixing strength work with conditioning under fatigue
- Complementary exercises: front rack walking lunges, Bulgarian split squats (tempo 3-1-1), reverse lunges

---

### 6.7 SkiErg — 1000m

**Pull Mechanics:**
- "Initiate with your lats, not your arms—imagine pulling your elbows down to your hips"
- The movement is a hip-hinge exercise combined with simultaneous downward elbow drive
- Approximately 40% of the movement's power comes from lower body and core stabilization
- Allow torso to hinge forward naturally with each stroke

**Core Engagement:**
- Brace core throughout the pulling motion
- Avoid hunched posture—engaged core stabilizes the hinge
- Drive handles fully to the floor by bending the legs (partial squat)

**Stroke Rate and Quality:**
- Aim for 30–35 strokes per minute (efficient, not frantic)
- Long, powerful strokes prioritize efficiency over speed
- Avoid choppy, abbreviated strokes—they waste energy

**Breathing Pattern:**
- Exhale during the pulling phase (effort)
- Inhale during recovery as arms rise
- One breath cycle per stroke maintains consistency

**Pacing Strategy (3-phase approach):**
1. Opening (0–300m): 70–75% effort; 1:55–2:05/500m splits; allow HR to settle after opening run
2. Build (300–700m): 80–85% effort; 1:50–2:00/500m splits; find sustainable rhythm
3. Finish (700–1000m): 90%+ effort; 1:45–1:55/500m; push knowing sled push follows

**Common errors to avoid:** Arm-only pulling, death-grip tension on handles, excessively fast starts, choppy strokes, breath-holding.

---

### 6.8 Rowing — 1000m

**Drive Sequence (Three-Part Power Sequence):**
1. Push with legs first (leg drive generates initial force)
2. Lean back slightly with torso (hip extension provides momentum)
3. Finish with pull to chest (arm pull completes the stroke)

**Return (Recovery) Phase:**
- Reverse: arms → torso forward → legs compress
- Control the return—do not rush. A rushed return spikes HR unnecessarily
- Keep movements smooth and connected

**Critical Positioning Cues:**
- Start tall with neutral spine; do not round back or collapse chest
- Keep shins vertical at the catch
- Hinge from hips, not spine
- Avoid early arm pulls that shorten the stroke and reduce power

**The single most common mistake:** "Pulling with arms first—kills power" because it bypasses the leg drive that generates 60–70% of the stroke's force.

**Pacing Strategy:** "Treat the row as active recovery with intent." After four prior stations, the temptation is to sprint on the rower. Elite athletes use the row to regulate breathing and lock into rhythm while saving legs for subsequent stations.

**Stroke Rate by Level:**
| Level | SPM | 500m Split (Men) | 500m Split (Women) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beginner | 24–26 | 2:10–2:20 | 2:20–2:40 |
| Intermediate | 26–28 | 1:50–2:10 | 2:00–2:20 |
| Competitive | 28–30 | 1:50–2:00 | 2:00–2:10 |
| Elite/Pro | 30–32 | 1:40–1:50 | 1:50–2:00 |

**Start strategy:** Begin 5% easier than race pace target; build into the middle 600m; finish stronger in final 200m.

## SECTION 7: PROGRAMMING RATIOS & VOLUME

### 7.1 Training Distribution Ratios

**Overall Training Time Distribution:**
- Running: ~50–60% of total training time (reflects race composition: 59% running, 41% functional stations)
- Functional station training: ~25–30%
- Pure strength work (barbell/accessory): ~15–20%
- Mobility/recovery: incorporated into all sessions

**Intensity Distribution (polarized model, evidence-based):**
- Zone 2 (easy): 80% of all training volume
- Zone 3 (moderate/tempo): ~5% (avoid over-using the gray zone)
- Zone 4–5 (high intensity): ~15–20%

**Functional Modality Breakdown (within station training time):**
- Sled work: 20–25% of station training time (high neurological demand; limit frequency)
- Carries (farmers + sandbag): 25–30% (high carry-over to race; tolerable frequency)
- Bodyweight metabolic (burpees, wall balls): 25–30% (high HR cost; program carefully)
- Accessory strength (lunges, squats, pulls): 20–25% (supportive role)

---

### 7.2 Reps, Sets, and Volume for Functional Exercises

**By Phase:**

| Phase | Focus | Rep Range | Load | Sets |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base/Accumulation | Technique + endurance | 10–15 reps | 50–60% race weight | 3–4 sets |
| Build/Transmutation | Strength + endurance | 4–8 reps strength; 15–25 functional | 70–85% | 4–5 sets |
| Peak/Realization | Race-specific | Race distance/reps | 100% race weight | 2–3 sets |
| Taper | Maintenance | Reduced reps/rounds | 80–90% | 1–2 sets |

**Specific Volume Benchmarks:**
- Sled: ≤150m total volume per session in base phase; up to 250m in build phase
- Wall balls: 50–75 reps per session in base phase; 100 reps (race standard) in peak phase
- Sandbag lunges: 20–40m in base; 50–80m in build; 100m (race standard) in peak
- Farmers carry: 3×40–60m in base; 3×100–150m in build; 1×200m in peak
- Burpee broad jumps: 15–20m sets in base; 40–60m in build; 80m in peak

---

### 7.3 Circuit vs. Straight Sets vs. Supersets for Hyrox

**Straight Sets (for strength development):**
- Best for: Base phase, building raw force capacity for sled, building carry strength
- Rest: 2–3 minutes between sets
- Example: 4×5 heavy sled push, full rest between

**Supersets (for efficiency + complementary development):**
- Best for: Build phase, combining upper and lower body work
- Example: Sled pull + SkiErg (paired upper body pull movements)
- Example: Sandbag lunges + farmers carry (leg + grip endurance)
- Rest: 60–90 seconds between pairs

**Circuit Training (for race simulation + metabolic conditioning):**
- Best for: Peak phase, developing tolerance for consecutive station fatigue
- Structure: Multiple stations performed back-to-back with minimal rest, then full rest between rounds
- Example: 800m run → sled push → wall balls × 25 → farmers carry × 100m → 3-min rest × 3 rounds
- The key: transition speed between stations also needs to be trained

**EMOM (Every Minute on the Minute):**
- Best for: Pacing practice, building station endurance at controlled intensity
- Example: Every 2 minutes for 20 minutes: 10 wall balls + 20m sandbag lunge
- Forces consistent pacing without relying on motivation-based effort

**Compromised Running Blocks (race-specific):**
- Best for: Final 8–12 weeks before race
- Structure: 1 km run → 1–2 stations → 1 km run → 1–2 different stations × 3–4 rounds
- Progress by: adding rounds, reducing rest, or increasing station load

---

### 7.4 Time-Based vs. Rep-Based Programming

**Time-based programming advantages for Hyrox:**
- Scales automatically to athlete capacity
- Better for teaching pacing under fatigue (athletes learn to manage effort over time)
- Useful for wall balls (timed sets reveal pace: how many reps in 2 minutes?)
- Best for compromised running blocks

**Rep-based programming advantages:**
- Clearer progression tracking
- Better for strength-focused station work
- Easier to monitor load-per-rep progression
- Better for carries (where stopping and restarting is the key variable)

**Coaching recommendation:** Use rep-based programming in the base and build phases for load tracking; transition to time-based and distance-based in the peak phase to replicate race conditions.

---

### 7.5 Optimal Session Duration for Hyrox Functional Training

**Session Duration by Type:**

| Session Type | Optimal Duration |
|---|---|
| Pure strength (straight sets) | 45–60 minutes |
| Station technique session | 45–55 minutes |
| Compromised running simulation | 45–75 minutes |
| Full race simulation | 85–110 minutes (race duration) |
| Zone 2 run only | 45–90 minutes |
| Combined run + stations | 60–80 minutes |

**Research context:** The 2025 scientific study found average Hyrox race completion time of 86.5 minutes (51 minutes running, 33 minutes stations). Full race simulations should therefore target this duration 1–2 times in the peak phase.

**Deload sessions:** 20–30 minutes Zone 1 movement (walk, easy bike, gentle mobility). Even on rest days, 20–30 minutes of low-intensity movement accelerates recovery versus complete sedentary rest.

## KEY COACHING PRINCIPLES SUMMARY

1. **Running is the race.** It consumes 59% of race time and is the largest single performance driver. VO2max is the #1 predictor of Hyrox finish time. Never neglect running for station work.

2. **Station work is loaded after cardio, not before.** Race conditions demand force output under fatigue. Training strength fresh does not prepare athletes for the race. The final 4 weeks must include sessions where stations follow hard running.

3. **The gray zone is the enemy.** Most Hyrox athletes train at moderate intensity (Zone 3) too frequently. The optimal distribution is polarized: 80% easy (Zone 2), 20% hard (Zone 4–5). Zone 3 creates fatigue without driving the adaptations needed for Hyrox.

4. **Progressive overload is multi-dimensional.** Load, volume, fatigue state, and race proximity are all valid progression variables. Do not only add weight—progress by training under fatigue, increasing race specificity, and reducing rest.

5. **Sled work is uniquely valuable and uniquely dangerous.** Its concentric-only nature makes it ideal for early return from injury and for high-frequency training. Its Achilles/calf loading makes it dangerous when overused. Twice weekly is the ceiling for most athletes.

6. **RPE in functional training requires interpretation.** Local muscular fatigue (grip, quad failure) is not the same as global fatigue. Athletes and coaches must learn to distinguish which system is failing and train accordingly.

7. **Periodization is non-negotiable.** Year-round high intensity destroys adaptation. Every 4 weeks, a deload is required. Every training year needs a base, build, peak, taper, and transition phase.

8. **Return from injury: modify, do not pause.** Athletes who maintain fitness through alternative modalities (SkiErg, rowing, cycling) during injury recovery return significantly fitter than those who rest completely. Targeted loading of injured structures (especially tendinopathies) is often a core component of rehabilitation.
